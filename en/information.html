The Information Patients Needed

The interviews showed that patients used many sources to find information about treatment and daily life. These included explanations from doctors and nurses, public-sector websites, hospital materials, books, patient groups, social media, and blogs and videos shared by people with similar experiences.

In Patients' Own Words

Information online made me more anxious because I came across frightening stories that were not even relevant to me. I searched for and borrowed books about ovarian cancer from the library, and trusted books most. I also looked at wig-related websites and Gannote, a YouTube channel featuring people's experiences of cancer. Through Gannote, I learned that patient groups existed. I searched for an ovarian cancer group and found SMILEY. In communities for people with gynecological cancers, such as Peer Ring, it was reassuring to be understood without having to explain everything. People sent comments and encouragement when treatment was difficult.

Patient A

My main sources of information were explanations from my doctor and nurses, reliable public-sector websites such as the National Cancer Center's, and patient brochures from the university hospital. Social media seemed full of worrying information, such as recurrence rates. My doctor advised me not to look if it made me anxious, so I began muting related posts. I have not joined a patient group, but I follow accounts run by organizations that share reliable information and use them as a reference. I deliberately avoid interacting with other patients because I am concerned about the emotional effect it might have on me.

Patient B

Much of my information comes from Instagram posts and direct messages from people who have also experienced ovarian cancer or recurrence. Hearing about similar treatments and the ways they manage is very helpful. I also discuss concerns such as self-funded treatment with a group representative, whose advice I find convincing and trustworthy. At times, information recommending expensive treatments or tests has left me unsure what to do. Being able to call the nurses in the medical oncology department and the specialist oncology pharmacist at my new hospital helped me regain perspective. They have been a tremendous source of support.

Patient C

I started looking for information after I returned home from my first chemotherapy treatment, beginning with blogs written by people with ovarian cancer. I have not joined a patient group, but I read the information they share and follow ovarian cancer awareness posts. I have never attended an in-person gathering with other patients or a Zoom seminar. Information about bowel stomas made me very anxious. However, I was still in touch with a healthcare professional I knew through a previous job, who told me, "You don't need to prepare anything. Just come with an open mind." That was a great comfort. The outpatient and ward nurses and doctors at the medical center have continued to support me.

Patient D

I gathered information about treatment mainly online and through books, including those recommended by the patient group SMILEY. I learned about SMILEY fairly soon after my diagnosis and joined some of their informal gatherings. I really appreciated hearing about the latest ovarian cancer information and new books. When treatment is not going as I had hoped, I become anxious and sometimes feel tempted by questionable treatments not covered by insurance, or unreliable claims that cancer can be cured by changing your diet or mindset. That has made me realize just how important it is to have access to accurate medical information from the very beginning. The nurse who cared for me in hospital was also a tremendous emotional support. And with the other patients with gynecological cancers I met on the ward, I sometimes felt a bond even deeper than with family.

Patient E

When I first learned I had cancer, I was too frightened to look for information myself. Once treatment had settled down and I was being monitored, I felt ready to read other people's blogs and find out what treatments might be available if the cancer returned. I then came across Ms. Katagi, the patient group representative, talking about surgery when her own cancer recurred. It closely reflected the situation I was struggling with and was a turning point in helping me wholeheartedly accept the plan to have chemotherapy rather than surgery this time. I mainly follow SMILEY on Instagram and have not attended their events, but I once sent a comment thanking them when my doubts about my treatment plan had cleared. There is so much information about treatments, supplements, high-dose vitamin C, and dietary therapies that it can be confusing. In the end, I tried to face the reality of my situation and make considered decisions for myself. The hospital pharmacist's consistently careful explanations were a great source of emotional support during treatment. That experience also inspired me to take training for a cancer patient support group. I now take part in peer support, listening to others who are struggling with the same illness.

Patient F

Finding information
and learning from others' experiences

Choosing how much
information to take in

Online and on social media, patients can easily encounter information that heightens fears about recurrence or prognosis, treatments with uncertain medical evidence, and personal stories. Searching for information could itself become a source of anxiety. Some patients therefore chose not to view cancer-related posts or limited their searches to sources they trusted.

Support from people
who have been through it

Patients wanted more than accurate medical information. They also wanted to hear from people with firsthand experience about managing side effects, adapting daily life during treatment, talking to doctors, and choosing treatment after recurrence. Connecting with someone who had experienced the same disease brought the reassurance of being understood without having to explain everything.

Reliable medical information
and lived experience

Both reliable information from healthcare professionals and public institutions, and practical, everyday insights from people with lived experience, are important. These accounts also suggest the value of being able to turn to a trusted healthcare professional or support organization, so that alarming or poorly substantiated information does not lead patients astray.
