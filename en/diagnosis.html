The Road to Diagnosis

Each patient's road to an ovarian cancer diagnosis began differently. In these interviews, changes they noticed in everyday life led them to seek medical care: severe period pain, sudden abdominal swelling, a feeling of heaviness in the lower abdomen, bloating, leg pain or swelling, or an enlarged ovary identified during screening.

In Patients' Own Words

Around October 2024, my period pain was worse than usual, so I went to the gynecology clinic I had been attending. A pelvic examination showed that an ovary was enlarged, and after an MRI the following day, I was referred to a university hospital. There, I had laparoscopic surgery for what were thought to be an ovarian cyst and uterine fibroids. About two weeks after surgery, the pathology results showed that I had ovarian cancer. The cancer had not been identified on the MRI, and I ended up needing two operations. I still think, "If only we had known sooner."

Patient A

Around the end of September 2024, my husband noticed that my abdomen had suddenly become so swollen that I looked pregnant. Two or three days later, I went to the clinic where I had previously had gastroscopies and colonoscopies. An ultrasound showed a clear abnormality, and I was referred to a local public hospital. After a series of tests, I was told that ovarian cancer was very likely. I was then referred to a university hospital that could offer clinical trials and major surgery. A laparoscopic examination ultimately confirmed the diagnosis of ovarian cancer.

Patient B

In December 2023, I had pain in my right groin. Ovarian cancer did not cross my mind, so I first went to an orthopedic clinic. An MRI showed that the problem was an enlarged ovary rather than my leg, and my regular gynecologist referred me to a large local hospital. From several possible hospitals, I then chose a cancer center. At first, I was told it was probably something benign, perhaps just an endometriotic cyst. However, a rapid pathology examination during surgery and the pathology results afterward showed that it was ovarian cancer.

Patient C

I had a heavy feeling in my lower abdomen, bloating, weight gain, and diarrhea every morning. About two weeks later, I went to my usual gynecology clinic. I was sent for an MRI immediately after a pelvic examination, and the results suggested that it might be malignant. I was referred to the medical center where I am treated now. Further tests followed, but my pain worsened and fluid was building up in my abdomen. I was admitted as an emergency and had emergency surgery. Based on the pathology results afterward, the plan was to start chemotherapy promptly. I was told that the cancer was stage 3C.

Patient D

I had always had painful periods, and both my mother and my maternal grandmother had had uterine conditions. At one point, my left leg became very swollen. I went to a doctor because I thought I might have a blood clot, and an MRI confirmed one. I was admitted to an affiliated hospital. While I was being treated for the clot, more detailed tests showed that there was abnormal ovarian enlargement. My tumor marker levels and other findings suggested that it could be cancer. I then had to find a hospital specializing in gynecological cancers myself. I contacted the health advice service provided by my life insurance company, and they referred me to a specialist hospital. After being admitted and having tests there, I underwent surgery. Pathology examination of the tissue removed during surgery confirmed ovarian cancer.

Patient E

In the fall of 2019, I went to a nearby gynecology clinic for the city's uterine cancer screening program and was told that my left ovary was enlarged. My tumor marker level was low, so the decision was to monitor it. At my appointment in December, the ovary was a little larger, but the marker level had fallen further. My doctor said, "This shows that it isn't malignant," which reassured me. However, around May 2020, I felt pain in my side and went back. The ovary had grown further, so I had it removed through laparoscopic surgery. After the pathology examination, my doctor told me, "It actually was malignant." That was when I first learned that I had ovarian cancer.

Patient F

From "It can't be cancer"
to a diagnosis

Different symptoms,
different starting points

The signs that led to diagnosis varied from patient to patient: sudden abdominal swelling, heaviness in the lower abdomen, bloating, leg pain or swelling, or an enlarged ovary identified during screening. The patients had not suspected ovarian cancer at the outset. Their paths to diagnosis began in different places, including a gynecology clinic, their regular clinic, an orthopedic clinic, or a screening appointment.

Tests and the
initial assessment

After seeking care, patients underwent tests such as pelvic examinations, ultrasound, MRI, and tumor marker testing, followed by referral to a hospital able to provide specialist investigations or surgery. In some cases, imaging and tumor markers alone did not lead to a finding of malignancy; a benign tumor or ovarian cyst was initially suspected.

A confirmed diagnosis
and the shock of the news

For many patients, ovarian cancer was ultimately confirmed through pathology examination during or after surgery, or through diagnostic laparoscopy. Some went through several tests and visited multiple healthcare providers before reaching a diagnosis. Symptoms that were difficult to recognize, and the uncertainty along the way, contributed to the shock of hearing that it was cancer.
