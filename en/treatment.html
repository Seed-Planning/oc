Treatment Choices After Diagnosis

Many patients felt deeply shocked and overwhelmed when they were told they had ovarian cancer. They described how difficult it was to take in such unexpected news: "My mind went blank," "It felt as though they were talking about someone else," and "I can barely remember what I was told."

In Patients' Own Words

After I was diagnosed with ovarian cancer, I was told that I would need further surgery following the removal of one ovary. The plan was an open operation to remove the remaining ovary, my uterus, fallopian tubes, and the lymph nodes in my groin, followed by chemotherapy. I understood this to be the standard treatment. After also talking with my mother and the gynecologist who had referred me, I put my trust in my treating doctor and went ahead. I had six treatments with paclitaxel plus carboplatin, followed by Avastin as maintenance therapy. I asked about side effects, hair loss, and whether I could be cured. My doctor answered my questions properly, and I felt I could trust them.

Patient A

The plan was to start with three cycles of carboplatin plus paclitaxel, assess how well it worked, and then operate. My doctor explained that this was the basic approach. Because the doctor had considerable expertise in ovarian cancer, I trusted them and accepted it without hesitation. With my husband there too, I asked about the medicines' side effects and how long the operation would take, and received careful explanations.

Patient B

For my initial treatment, the cancer center offered only TC chemotherapy. There was no explanation of other options, and the atmosphere made it difficult to ask questions, so I did not feel fully comfortable with the plan. After six cycles of TC chemotherapy, I had maintenance therapy. But I lost confidence in how rising tumor markers and suspected recurrence were handled. I sought a second opinion and transferred to a municipal hospital some distance away. There, I received various treatments and was able to move forward with a doctor who understood the patient's perspective and was easy to talk to. The current plan is to continue treatment at a university hospital.

Patient C

My initial treatment was supposed to be carboplatin plus paclitaxel, but I had an anaphylactic reaction during the first administration. The second was therefore given more slowly. After that, because the pain and other symptoms were severe, treatment continued mainly with carboplatin. I have been through chemotherapy before surgery, a major operation lasting 12 hours, chemotherapy after surgery, maintenance therapy, and various further treatments after recurrence. I am now continuing with Avastin alone. My doctor has mainly proposed the treatment plans, and I have moved forward after listening to the explanations, even when I did not fully understand everything. I am cared for by a team, not just one doctor. Having doctors and nurses I can trust gives me reassurance.

Patient D

Even before surgery, I received detailed explanations about the likely stage of my cancer, the six months of chemotherapy with carboplatin and paclitaxel that would follow, the expected side effects, and the recovery process. It had all happened so suddenly that I could not think of any questions. I felt, "I'll leave everything to the doctor." Looking back, though, I wish I had asked more about my exact cancer type, other treatment options, and what would happen if the first chemotherapy did not work. My tumor markers rose after the initial treatment, so I went on to radiotherapy, then another medicine, Doxil, and now Keytruda. My main doctor changed along the way, but the hospital as a whole has a steady, reassuring atmosphere, and I feel well cared for by the team. That is why I keep going there, even though it is far from home.

Patient E

At my first appointment at the university hospital, I was told that surgery would remove all the disease that could be removed, including my remaining ovary, uterus, and fallopian tubes. Chemotherapy afterward would target cancer cells too small to see. At the time, I found it a little difficult to ask my doctor questions, and being in a large hospital made it harder too. At first, I simply accepted the treatment plan I was given. But I also felt strongly that I wanted to do everything possible to save my life. After surgery, I had six treatments with TC chemotherapy, followed by a break from treatment while I was monitored. In 2023, however, the cancer returned in two places: in the lymph nodes and beside the rectum. I said I wanted it removed surgically, but was told surgery would be difficult because it had recurred in two or more places. I chose chemotherapy with TC and Avastin instead. I now continue to take a medicine called Lynparza. During the break from treatment, I began learning more about the disease myself. Now I can have proper discussions with my doctor and feel comfortable with the treatment we are following.

Patient F

Understanding treatment
and what lies ahead in daily life

Shock and confusion
after diagnosis

At diagnosis, patients are given a great deal of information at once: the cancer stage, pathology results, the planned surgery, chemotherapy, maintenance therapy, side effects, and what to expect. Yet their concerns extend beyond medical information. Questions such as "How will my life change?" and "Will I be able to keep working and living with my family as before?" also matter greatly.

Making sense of the plan
and taking an active role

Some patients trusted the standard treatment proposed by their doctor and were able to focus on the treatment ahead. Others felt they had not been able to ask enough questions or wanted to hear more about other options. As they experienced recurrence, side effects, decisions about medicines, second opinions, or changes of hospital, some began gathering information themselves and taking a more active role in their care.

A supportive environment
and a sense of reassurance

Soon after diagnosis, patients need not only clear explanations of their treatment plan, but also an environment where they feel safe discussing worries and questions. Doctors and nurses answering questions, family members being present for explanations, and access to second opinions or patient support information when needed helped patients feel more comfortable and reassured.
