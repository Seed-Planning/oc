Fear of Recurrence and Sources of Support

Worries do not necessarily disappear once a course of treatment is over. In these interviews, patients spoke about fear of recurrence, the tension of waiting for test results, concerns about tumor marker levels and scans, and uncertainty about future treatment choices.

In Patients' Own Words

I am continuing Avastin to help prevent recurrence, and I still feel anxious about being in treatment and not knowing how I will feel from day to day. The numbness in my toes remains, and I have been told that it may never disappear completely. Having cancer has made me realize that feeling well is not something to take for granted. I want to make every day count. Even during a difficult period, I think it is important to do things you enjoy when you feel well enough, and to give yourself something that lifts your spirits. Once treatment settles down, I hope to return to a permanent, full-time job.

Patient A

Information online sometimes made me more afraid of recurrence. But my doctor explained that even if the cancer came back, there would still be treatment options, and that some patients in a similar situation had remained well for a long time. That has been a great source of reassurance. After six cycles of chemotherapy following surgery, we are also considering maintenance therapy or a clinical trial, depending on my blood test results. I would like people around me to understand that I have good days and bad days. When I have questions, I try not to research them endlessly myself, but ask my doctor or nurses directly. Planning things I enjoy, such as concerts and trips, motivates me to keep going with treatment.

Patient B

Every time the cancer comes back, I feel a deep sense of despair. I keep wondering why it returns when I am having treatment at such short intervals. Not being able to join a clinical trial was another major shock. But posts from a patient who keeps going despite four recurrences, and support from people I met at a patient support group, have encouraged me. I will continue treatment at a university hospital and am hopeful about new treatment options. I wish the same medicines were available to everyone around the world. More than anything, I want to get well so that I can give something back to my family for all their support.

Patient C

At the end of 2024, I was told that the cancer had returned in my lower right abdomen. Surgery was not possible, so I had chemotherapy. The tumor is not growing now, and radiotherapy may be keeping it under control. We therefore stopped the chemotherapy that was causing allergic reactions, and the plan is to continue Avastin alone for as long as the side effects allow. I am very anxious about my monthly tumor marker results and my CT scans. But as long as things remain stable, including my thyroid treatment, I would like to continue, accepting some pain along the way. Even before I became ill, I valued the idea of finding something positive in a difficult situation. I still try to look for what has gone well, even when something bad happens. If sharing my experience could help someone else, I would be very happy to do so.

Patient D

My tumor marker level rose soon after my first chemotherapy, TC treatment, and it was a shock to learn that the medicine had not worked. It also made me realize that I had not thought enough about the characteristics of my cancer or what other treatment options might come next. After treatment with another medicine, Doxil, my tumor markers rose again. However, earlier pathology and genetic testing had already shown that I was eligible for Keytruda, so I was able to move straight on to treatment every three weeks. At one point, abnormal liver function results meant I had to take a break for about six months, but I have now restarted treatment. I sincerely hope that future care will offer more treatments and oral medicines with fewer side effects and less need for hospital visits or stays. Above all, I believe the most important thing is to use accurate information to choose appropriate care that suits you, and to continue with it.

Patient E

When I learned that the cancer had returned, I was torn between my strong wish to have it removed surgically and my doctor's judgment that surgery was not appropriate this time. But as I gathered information and learned about other people's experiences, I found that I could accept the plan. I told my doctor, "I trust you, and I'll go ahead with this treatment." I am now continuing with oral Lynparza, while also preparing myself for the possibility of returning to intravenous chemotherapy in the future. When people see me looking well and working, they often seem to assume that the cancer is cured and belongs in the past. It is hard to convey the fear of recurrence and what it really means to remain in treatment, and that can feel a little lonely. What keeps me looking ahead is my wish to see my students grow tomorrow and to stay with them until they graduate, along with my strong desire to keep doing the work I love. For the future, I hope not only for new, more effective medicines, but also for prices that reduce the financial burden so people can continue treatment while working, and for open access to information that anyone can easily find whenever they need it.

Patient F

Fear of recurrence
and life ahead

Treatment ends,
but the worries remain

Faced with recurrence or ongoing treatment, patients found their own ways to cope: asking doctors and nurses questions, researching information, and drawing support from other patients. Yet some felt that those around them saw the illness as something that was already over, making it difficult to communicate the worries that continued after treatment.

What matters to you,
and others' experiences,
can help you look ahead

Time with family, continuing to work, enjoying travel and concerts, connecting with people who had similar experiences, and wanting to use their own experiences to help someone else all gave patients strength to look ahead. Future goals and hopes, and seeing other patients keep going through repeated recurrences, were also powerful sources of support.

Reclaiming everyday life,
even with uncertainty

Life continues after treatment or recurrence, alongside physical and emotional concerns. Even while living with anxiety, patients were determined to reclaim their everyday lives, drawing strength from what mattered to them and from the goals they still hoped to reach.
