How Treatment Affects Daily Life

Surgery, drug treatment, and radiotherapy place a considerable burden on the body. In these interviews, patients described a wide range of physical effects, including pain and reduced stamina after surgery, fatigue, numbness, hair loss, nausea, mouth sores, joint and muscle pain, infections, and difficulties with bowel and bladder function.

In Patients' Own Words

After open surgery, I developed a bowel obstruction. The hardest part was spending four days with a tube through my nose and nothing to eat or drink. Lymphatic fluid also leaked from my vagina for about a month, and needing to wear diapers was a major burden. Chemotherapy caused nausea, constipation, loss of appetite, stomach pain, joint and muscle pain, reduced immunity, mouth sores, and hair loss. After each treatment, I was almost bedridden for about a week. I had to leave my job, and I still have less stamina and numbness in my toes. With those symptoms and the anxiety of still being in treatment, I find it hard to imagine working full-time, five days a week.

Patient A

During chemotherapy before surgery, I had hair loss and numbness, but few severe side effects such as nausea, and treatment was never postponed. I spent about a month in hospital for surgery. It was a major operation involving my liver and diaphragm, and a blood clot meant I had to stay longer. The hardest time was after surgery in the high-care unit, when I could not sleep surrounded by tubes and monitors. I have been able to keep working without losing my contracts. At home, we manage the housework with help from my husband, a housekeeping service, and both sides of the family.

Patient B

With my first TC chemotherapy, I had hair loss, numbness in my feet, and severe itching in my hands and feet, but less nausea than I had expected. During treatment for recurrence, my body began to feel lighter, and afterward I recovered enough to travel overseas with my daughter. Later treatments, however, caused severe problems with my mucous membranes, including mouth sores. I could not eat, and lost weight and strength. I have also been taken to hospital by ambulance because of fluid building up in my abdomen and a blocked ureter. Being on leave from work now makes it easier to manage side effects, but I sometimes feel isolated, as though the rest of the world is moving on without me.

Patient C

After my first operation, I was in a lot of pain and had lost so much strength that even stepping up into the entrance of my home was difficult. Because it had been major surgery, I needed a bowel stoma and had to catheterize myself. After about six months to a year, though, I could manage my bowel and bladder needs, and finally felt that I was really living again. After about six months off work, I returned gradually, starting with shorter hours and building up to full-time, with advice from the occupational physician, public health nurse, and human resources team. Daily hospital visits during radiotherapy were difficult, so I took a week off partway through. I still work with adjustments for tasks such as prolonged standing and heavy lifting.

Patient D

My first chemotherapy was the hardest treatment I have been through. Alongside hair loss and a round, puffy face, I had joint and muscle pain so severe that I could not get up. Nausea was not too bad, but the pain throughout my body was intense. I then had about 30 radiotherapy sessions. Each session was short, but the journey took one and a half to two hours each way and was physically exhausting, so I was admitted for the latter part of the course. Another medicine, Doxil, caused severe hand-foot syndrome. The skin on my feet peeled and became inflamed, so I could no longer wear ordinary shoes. Even at work, I wore sandals with my feet bandaged. I have used up almost all my paid leave for hospital visits, and often feel that my company's formal support is insufficient. Working continues to be a struggle.

Patient E

After my first operation, I developed an infection. My white blood cell count rose, and I felt terribly fatigued and unwell. The most difficult memory of surgery is not being allowed to drink water afterward. I was so desperately thirsty that, before my next operation, I asked the nurses to keep moistening my lips, at least. During chemotherapy, my body felt as heavy as lead. At first, even standing was hard, and I asked the principal at the school where I work whether I could teach sitting down. Strangely, though, I often felt the symptoms less at work, perhaps because I switched into work mode. In fact, during my second course of treatment, TC chemotherapy plus Avastin, I was able to keep working full-time from the day after each treatment. My husband's support made that possible. He took on all the housework, so when I got home, all I had to do was bathe and go to bed. I am truly grateful.

Patient F

The physical burden
and its emotional impact

Patients described pain and reduced stamina after surgery, fatigue, numbness, hair loss, nausea, mouth sores, joint and muscle pain, infections, and difficulties with bowel and bladder function. Experiences such as being unable to drink water, lying awake surrounded by tubes and monitors, or losing control over bowel and bladder function also brought anxiety, helplessness, and uncertainty about the future.

Work, workplace support,
and emotional challenges

Some patients left their jobs or took leave, some returned with adjusted hours, and others had to change the way they worked because of hospital visits or side effects. Practical and emotional challenges included managing paid leave, gaining understanding at work, and receiving appropriate adjustments when returning. Some also felt isolated during leave, as though society was moving on without them.

Changes at home
and support from others

At times, it became difficult to manage housework, childcare, and everyday personal tasks as before. Patients adapted with support from husbands and other family members, housekeeping services, occupational physicians, public health nurses, and human resources staff. This support helped ease not only the physical burden, but also anxiety and feelings of isolation during treatment.
